# Climbing Hydrangea

**Scientific name:** Hydrangea anomala petiolaris  
**Swedish:** Klätterhortensia  
**Polish:** Hortensja pnąca

> **Confirmed June 2026:** White flowers on elongated panicles observed, consistent with Hydrangea anomala petiolaris. Red new stems and serrated leaves also match.

## Basic Information

Climbing hydrangea is one of the few climbers that thrives in shade and can scale walls without support, attaching via aerial rootlets. It's a vigorous, long-lived deciduous climber that builds up a thick framework of woody stems over the years. In the back garden it covers the terrace wall, providing texture and greenery against the fence.

### Characteristics
- **Type:** Deciduous climbing shrub
- **Height:** 8–15 meters (if given a tall surface to climb)
- **Spread:** 3–5 meters from the wall
- **Growth rate:** Slow for the first 2–3 years, then fast once established
- **Flowers:** White lacecap clusters (10–25 cm across) in June–July
- **Foliage:** Broadly ovate, serrated leaves (5–10 cm), dark green with prominent veining
- **Bark:** Peeling, cinnamon-brown on mature stems; red-tinged new growth
- **Lifespan:** 50+ years

### Growing Conditions
- **Light:** Partial shade to full shade (one of the best climbers for shade); tolerates full sun
- **Soil:** Moist, well-drained, humus-rich; prefers slightly acidic to neutral
- **pH:** 5.5–7.0
- **Hardiness:** Zone 4–8 (fully hardy in the Stockholm area)

## Location Notes

Climbing hydrangea is well-suited to the Stockholm climate:
- Fully winter-hardy; no protection required
- Excellent for the shaded terrace wall — one of the few flowering climbers that performs well in shade
- Attaches to walls and fences via aerial rootlets — no trellis needed, but can also scramble over structures
- Heavy plant once mature — ensure the fence or wall can support the weight
- The dense framework provides sheltered nesting spots for birds

## Monthly Care Calendar

### January
- **Tasks:** None required
- **Watch for:** Plant is dormant; the peeling bark and woody framework add winter texture

### February
- **Tasks:**
  - This is the best time to prune out dead wood and thin the framework (see Pruning Guide)
  - Plan any major renovation pruning before new growth starts
- **Watch for:** Buds beginning to swell in late February during mild spells

### March
- **Tasks:**
  - Complete any structural pruning before leaves emerge
  - Remove dead, damaged, or congested stems
- **Watch for:** Fresh leaf buds opening; new red-tinged shoots appearing

### April
- **Tasks:**
  - Apply balanced slow-release fertilizer around the base
  - Mulch with täckbark brun (5–8 cm layer), keeping mulch away from the main stems
  - Water if spring is dry, especially younger plants
- **Watch for:** Rapid new growth; leaves unfurling

### May
- **Tasks:**
  - Water during dry spells
  - Guide any wayward shoots back toward the wall if needed
  - Light pruning to shape if necessary
- **Watch for:** Flower buds forming; vigorous leafy growth

### June
- **Tasks:**
  - Enjoy the flowering display (lacecap white flowers if this is indeed climbing hydrangea)
  - **Check flower colour to confirm identification**
  - Water regularly during dry weather
- **Watch for:** Peak flowering — note the flower colour and shape for ID confirmation

### July
- **Tasks:**
  - Prune immediately after flowering if needed — shorten outward-growing shoots
  - Water during prolonged dry spells
  - Light shaping is fine this month
- **Watch for:** Flowers fading; seed heads forming

### August
- **Tasks:**
  - Stop major pruning
  - Water during dry periods
- **Watch for:** Growth rate slowing; plant consolidating for the season

### September
- **Tasks:**
  - No pruning — avoid stimulating soft growth before winter
  - Leave dried flower heads for winter interest
- **Watch for:** Leaves beginning to turn yellow

### October
- **Tasks:**
  - Top up mulch around the base (5–8 cm)
  - Deep water before ground freezes if autumn has been dry
- **Watch for:** Autumn leaf colour (yellow); leaves dropping

### November
- **Tasks:**
  - Clear fallen leaves from around the base
  - Check that the plant's weight isn't damaging the fence
- **Watch for:** Fully dormant; woody framework and peeling bark visible

### December
- **Tasks:** None required
- **Watch for:** Appreciate the winter silhouette and bark texture

## Pruning Guide

### Why This Plant Needs Pruning
Climbing hydrangea builds up a thick mat of old woody stems against its support. Without occasional thinning, the dead wood accumulates (as currently visible on the terrace wall), the plant gets too heavy, and new growth only appears at the outer edges.

### When to Prune
- **Dead wood removal:** Late winter (February–March), before new growth
- **Shaping and thinning:** After flowering (July)
- **Avoid:** Pruning in autumn — soft new growth is vulnerable to frost

### Dealing with Dead Wood (Current Situation)

The dense tangle of dry, grey stems is typical for an established climbing hydrangea that hasn't been thinned recently:

1. **Start in late winter or now (spring is also fine)**
2. **Identify dead stems** — dry, brittle, grey/brown, no buds or green tissue
3. **Cut dead stems back** to where they meet live wood, or remove entirely at the base
4. **Work carefully** — avoid pulling stems out of the tangle, which can damage live growth; cut and remove in sections
5. **Thin the worst congestion** but leave the main live framework attached to the wall
6. **Clear debris** from the base of the wall

### Annual Maintenance Pruning

After flowering (July):

1. **Shorten outward-growing shoots** — cut back branches that stick out too far from the wall to 2–3 buds
2. **Remove crossing or rubbing stems**
3. **Thin congested areas** to improve air circulation
4. **Keep the plant within bounds** — cut back growth encroaching on windows, gutters, or the roof

### Renovation Pruning
If the plant is severely overgrown:
- Cut back hard to the main framework in February–March
- The plant will skip flowering for one season but recover strongly
- Feed well after hard pruning

## Seasonal Considerations

### Before Spring (January–March)
- Best time to tackle the accumulated dead wood
- Structural pruning before growth starts
- The framework provides winter interest even when bare

### Spring–Summer (April–August)
- Vigorous growth period — the plant fills in quickly
- Flowering in June–July is the highlight
- Prune for shape after flowering

### Autumn (September–November)
- No pruning; let the plant harden for winter
- Yellow autumn leaf colour
- Leave dried flower heads for visual interest

### Winter (December–February)
- Fully hardy — no protection needed in Stockholm
- Peeling bark and woody structure have architectural appeal
- Plan pruning for late February

## Common Problems in Swedish Climate

### Pests
- **Aphids:** Occasionally on new growth; rarely serious
- **Scale insects:** Can appear on old woody stems; scrape off or treat with horticultural oil
- **Slugs and snails:** May damage young growth at the base
- **Generally very pest-resistant**

### Diseases
- **Leaf spot:** Brown spots in wet summers; cosmetic only
- **Powdery mildew:** White coating on leaves in dry, still conditions; improve air circulation by thinning
- **Honey fungus:** Rare but serious; causes dieback — remove affected parts

### Environmental Issues
- **No flowers:** Usually means the plant is still young (can take 3–5 years to start flowering) or is being pruned at the wrong time
- **Too much dead wood:** Normal accumulation — thin regularly in late winter
- **Pulling away from wall:** Heavy mature growth can detach; re-attach or prune back heavy sections
- **Slow to establish:** Normal — climbing hydrangea is famously slow in its first few years, then grows vigorously

## Tips for Success

1. **Clean out dead wood regularly** — a late-winter thinning every year or two prevents the tangled buildup currently visible on the terrace wall

2. **Be patient with young plants** — the saying goes "first year it sleeps, second year it creeps, third year it leaps"

3. **Don't prune before flowering** — flowers form on the previous year's wood; prune only after blooming

4. **Check the support** — a mature climbing hydrangea is heavy; ensure the fence can take the weight, or thin to reduce load

5. **Mulch the base** — the roots prefer cool, moist conditions; a layer of bark mulch helps

6. **Confirm the ID** — watch for flowers in June–July; if they're yellow rather than white, this may be a different species and the care guide should be revised
